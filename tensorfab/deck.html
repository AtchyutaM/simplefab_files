A differentiable production line

We rewrote the fab simulator as a batched tensor program so that automatic differentiation can trace scheduling decisions all the way to profit. This deck covers what was built, what it proved, what it did not, and whether the approach earns the next round of investment.

All figures final. The CP-SAT column is a 600-second solve reporting both its best schedule and its dual bound — a rigorous ceiling no schedule can exceed. An earlier 180-second solve appeared to show the gradient beating the optimiser on one pattern; that was an artifact of an unconverged solve and does not survive.

The verdict

The mechanism works, and it now has a margin

Confirmed

Gradients survive the discontinuities

Credit assignment propagates through 2,688 steps of hard threshold gates with zero non-finite values. More than half the gradient mass sits in the first quarter of the horizon — the signal reaches decisions made 2,000+ ticks before their revenue arrives.

Confirmed

It beats every search baseline

On all four real demand patterns, at equal budget from identical starts, and it lands within 3–8% of a certified upper bound. At 50% utilisation, where the optimum requires coordinated idling, the margin over zeroth-order search is an order of magnitude. The exact solver still edges it — see below.

Not yet shown

Nothing about closed-loop policies

Every result here optimises an open-loop schedule with full horizon information — the MILP's game, not PPO's. No reactive policy has been trained. That is the next experiment and the one that matters.

Result 3 — design findings

Three things that would have cost weeks to learn later

Exact forward beats relaxed forward

The fully-relaxed simulator (grad-soft) loses to the greedy heuristic on three of four patterns. Optimising a smoothed system optimises the wrong system, and rounding back to integers discards the gains. Straight-through — exact forward, surrogate backward — is the correct design.

The exact solver still wins — but slowly

CP-SAT beats the gradient on all four patterns, by $590 to $1,375. It needed 600 s to get there, and even then closed the optimality gap on none of them — 0.4% to 1.5% remained on a single-product, four-machine problem. At 180 s it had been beaten by random search. Solve time, not solution quality, is the variable to watch as the problem grows.

A live bug in the training reward

env.py sums four numpy booleans, and numpy boolean addition is logical OR — so the invalid-action count saturates at 1. With invalid_action_penalty=-2.0, an agent requesting four illegal starts is penalised exactly as much as one. Found by the port's equivalence test.

Against the record

How this compares to the previous experiments

Because the models behind the historical scores had been overwritten, PPO was retrained from scratch on all three patterns (8M steps each, unchanged hyperparameters) under today's cost settings, then evaluated with the project's own methodology — temperature 0.7, best of 20 episodes. Those retrains land within about ±$1,600 of the historical record, which for single-seed runs is unremarkable. The commander column is exactly reproducible: the tensor simulator reproduces all three published values to the dollar, which is what makes the rest of the comparison trustworthy.

Read the last two columns with care — they are not a fair fight

The gradient column optimises an open-loop schedule with full advance knowledge of demand — the same privilege the MILP has. PPO is a reactive policy that sees only an 18-dimensional observation and must decide tick by tick. Beating it here is expected and proves nothing about closed-loop performance. What the comparison does establish is a useful bound: it shows how much profit is on the table for a scheduler that reasons over the whole horizon, and therefore how much a reactive policy is giving up. The fair comparisons are gradient vs MILP (both open-loop) and PPO vs commander (both reactive).

The old "theoretical max" was not an optimum

It was a hand-computed bound — roughly demand × (revenue − production cost) minus a small term. Every "% of maximum" figure in the project record was measured against it. Replacing it with a certified CP-SAT dual bound on all four patterns is a standalone upgrade to the project's evaluation, independent of anything the gradient does.

What was built

Infrastructure, and what proves it correct

Three equivalence gates, all passing

  • Simulator — 28 trajectories × 4 demand modes × 2,688 ticks. Every queue count, machine state, shipment and cost accumulator identical; profit agrees to ~1e-9.
  • Environment — all 18 observation dimensions, action mask, reward, shaping term and invalid counts match across 3 shaping configurations.
  • Differentiable line — forward pass bit-identical to the exact simulator; naive autodiff through the hard gates returns exactly zero, as it must.

Throughput, XLA CPU only

No GPU numbers yet — macOS has no CUDA backend. The environment wrapper mattered more than the physics: it cost 4.4× the dynamics it wrapped.

The honest ledger

What is banked, and what is not

Banked

  • A verified differentiable simulator — a capability that did not exist
  • Experimental velocity: the four-pattern, five-method comparison ran in 16 minutes
  • The gradient beats every baseline on every real demand pattern
  • A certified optimality bound replacing a hand-computed one
  • A live reward bug in the training pipeline

Not banked

  • No policy is better. Best learned result is still the May PPO run
  • The gradient does not beat the exact solver — CP-SAT leads on all four patterns by $590–$1,375
  • No training speedup — PPO not yet swapped over; the simulator was never its bottleneck
  • Nothing tested beyond one product and four machines
  • Open-loop only — these schedules need full advance knowledge of demand
  • The scaling premise remains an assumption, not a measurement

Next

Three moves, in priority order

1  ·  Test the assumption everything rests on

Scale CP-SAT up — more machines, then multiple products, then reentrant flows — and chart where solve time explodes. The entire strategic case depends on exact optimisation breaking at realistic scale, and that is currently backed by a complexity-class argument rather than a measurement. Roughly a week, and it either validates or kills the premise before anything expensive is built. The 180-second failures above suggest the knee may be closer than assumed.

2  ·  The closed-loop gradient test

Train a reactive policy by backpropagating end-of-episode profit through the dynamics. Straight-through only; gradient clipping is mandatory. Pre-registered kill criterion: if it cannot beat PPO's $31,493 on Alternating 92%, the pathwise route is dead for this problem and we stop.

3  ·  A cheaper hypothesis, in parallel

PPO's diagnosed failure is discounted local reward driving under-production. The tensorised environment now makes it cheap to run PPO at large batch with γ=1.0 and full-episode returns, which attacks that pathology directly — no differentiability required. It would be embarrassing to build a differentiable trainer and then find the discount factor was the answer.

Research potential

An honest assessment

Where there is little

GPU-vectorised environments, differentiable simulation, straight-through estimators, and RL for job-shop scheduling are all well-trodden. "We built a fast simulator" is engineering, not a contribution.

Where there is real potential

  • Differentiable discrete-event simulation. The literature is overwhelmingly rigid-body and contact physics. Production and queueing systems are a genuine gap.
  • Why it works here. Our discontinuities are multiplicative gates, not reflective contacts — so the linear pathway survives underneath the surrogate. That is a mechanistic explanation for beating the pessimism in the literature.
  • The reentrancy crossover. Charting where exact solvers break against where a learned policy still trains would be a real contribution — and it is move 1 above.

Overall: moderate, and concentrated almost entirely in the scaling question. On a four-machine toy the problem is too easy for any optimiser to distinguish itself decisively — which is exactly what the 90%-utilisation results show.

Confirmed

On all four single-product demand patterns and all three two-product mixes, at equal budget from identical starts. The margin tracks one thing: how many genuine choices the schedule contains. It is an order of magnitude at 50% utilisation, $967–$2,664 across the product mixes, and negative where the line runs saturated and there is nothing left to choose.

Not shown

CP-SAT returns a better schedule than every learned method on every configuration tested — seven for seven. The gradient reaches 91–95% of a certified bound but never passes it.

The tension in one sentence

Backorder costs roughly four times what finished-goods holding costs, so you want to build early — but the pipeline takes L ticks to fill and every tick of earliness is charged. The optimal release is a narrow window, and it sits L ticks before demand it cannot yet see.

Three ways to attack it

05 / 29

What we had, what failed, and what we built

1 · Discrete-event simulation

Python objects: a machine holds busy and finish_time; a queue is a list of arrival timestamps. One trajectory at a time, one tick per loop iteration. Exact, readable, and the reference every other implementation is checked against — but it produces no gradient, and at 358k steps/s it is a hard ceiling on how much experience you can generate.

2 · Model-free RL — Maskable PPO

A policy network reads an 18-dim observation and picks run/idle per tool. It learns from a per-tick reward and a score-function gradient. Two problems: the reward is local, so a production decision shows its cost immediately and its revenue hundreds of ticks later, discounted; and the gradient is high-variance, needing 8–16M env steps. PPO reliably drifts into under-production and erodes its own behavioural-cloning prior.

3 · Tensorized & differentiable

The same dynamics rewritten as fixed arrays with a batch axis, so thousands of trajectories advance in lockstep and automatic differentiation can trace decisions to profit. Two payoffs: throughput (60M steps/s vs 358k), and a gradient of true end-of-horizon profit with respect to every decision — no local proxy, no discounting.

Why the third exists at all

Not because the simulator was too slow — it was never PPO's bottleneck. Because PPO's failure was diagnosed as reward misalignment: the signal it optimises is not the objective we want. Making the dynamics differentiable removes the proxy entirely. You stop asking "was this tick good?" and start asking "how does this decision change the profit at the end?"

The tensorization

06 / 29

How a discrete-event line becomes an array program

The reference simulator stores each queue as a list of timestamps and each tool as a (busy, finish_time) pair. Neither is differentiable, and neither batches. Only counts and countdowns affect the dynamics, so the entire state of B trajectories collapses to four arrays.

State

ArrayShapeMeaning
q[B, M]units waiting in each queue
reg[B, M, 17]delay register — jobs in flight
qfin[B]finished goods
backlog[B]demand not yet shipped

Plus integer accumulators for shipped units, starts, and the three holding costs. Prices are applied once at the end, so profit is a linear function of exact integers — no per-tick float rounding.

The delay register

A start writes a 1 into slot pt. The register shifts left one slot per tick. A job reaching slot 0 completes. "Busy" is just reg.sum() > 0, so one-job-at-a-time capacity is free.

Why a register and not a countdown: done = reg[...,0] is a pure selection, so the gradient flows from a start decision straight through to its completion. A countdown would need rem == 1, whose derivative is zero everywhere.

One tick, as five array operations

Ordering is load-bearing: completions run before starts, so a tool finishing at tick t can restart at t, and a unit deposited by tool m is available to tool m+1 in the same tick. Routing is a matrix product, which is what generalises the line to any depth — and to reentrancy, where a route simply points backwards.

Where the gradient comes from

07 / 29

Tracing a decision to the profit it causes

Every decision is a real-valued logit θ[t, m]. The question autodiff answers is ∂profit / ∂θ — and the answer travels the whole causal chain, not a one-tick reward.

The obstacle: three hard gates

A start happens only if action × idle × canrun. The action is binary, and canrun = 1[q ≥ take] is a step function — derivative zero almost everywhere, undefined at the jump. Differentiating the exact simulator returns exactly zero, which we verify as a control.

The third gate is nearly free: because a tool holds at most one job, idle = 1 − reg.sum() is exact and linear, so it needs no relaxation and contributes no bias.

The fix: exact forward, surrogate backward

Straight-through estimation. The forward pass uses the hard gate, so the simulation stays bit-identical to the reference. The backward pass substitutes a sigmoid. Written hard + (soft − stop_gradient(soft)) — the parenthesisation matters, since the obvious alternative is not exact in float32 and silently invented $230 of profit before it was caught.

Gradients are therefore biased but usable. That is inherent: you cannot have exact derivatives of a discontinuous function.

What this buys, and what it does not

It buys credit assignment across the whole horizon: at 2,688 ticks, more than half the gradient magnitude sits in the first quarter of the schedule, with zero non-finite values. The signal genuinely reaches decisions made 2,000+ ticks before the revenue they earn — exactly the decision PPO's discounted local reward gets wrong.

It does not buy an optimiser that always wins. The surrogate carries information only where the gates are live. Where the line runs saturated, the gates are shut almost always, the gradient says little, and simple random search beats it — which is precisely what the 92% results show.

Why "% of bound" is trustworthy before CP-SAT finishes

The dual bound is proven at every moment of a solve: it starts at the LP relaxation and only ever moves down toward the true optimum. It can never move up. So these percentages are conservative — a longer solve can only improve them.

It does need some solve time to become useful. At 20 seconds it is still at the trivial $26,880 (336 units × $80 — ship everything, pay nothing). By 45 seconds it has tightened to $19,008.0, and 600 seconds moves it only a further 0.02% to $19,004.2. So: tens of seconds to become meaningful, then stable.

The incumbent — CP-SAT's best actual schedule — behaves the opposite way: it takes tens of minutes and is the number still outstanding at depth.

What makes the comparison fair

  • Equal budget. 76,800 rollout-equivalents, counting one gradient step as three forward passes (forward + backward). The zeroth-order methods therefore get 1,200 iterations at batch 64.
  • Same starting point. Grad ST and Hill-climb begin from identical random tapes; only random search draws fresh samples.
  • Same yardstick. Every number is exact-integer-sim profit, never the relaxed objective a method optimised internally.
  • Not comparable to PPO. All of these optimise an open-loop schedule with full advance knowledge of demand. PPO is reactive and sees an observation vector. The fair pairings are gradient vs CP-SAT and PPO vs commander.

“50%” and “92%” of which capacity?

Demand is set from a fixed nominal capacity of 672 units = 0.25 units/tick x 2688 ticks, where 0.25 is one diffusion stage (4 units / 16 ticks). That figure is depth-independent by construction: adding stages adds tools, and steady-state throughput is set by the slowest single stage, not by how many follow it.

Demand is constant in units, not in percent

Every depth receives the same absolute demand — 336 units in the 50% sweep, 616 in the 92% sweep. The percentage label is computed once against the depth-independent nominal figure of 672, so it is a convenient name for the experiment, not a per-line measurement.

Against what each line can actually deliver in a finite horizon, effective load rises with depth: the “50%” sweep runs at 50.6% → 52.5%, and the “92%” sweep at 92.8% → 96.2%. A deeper pipeline spends more of the horizon filling and draining, so fewer units clear it.

Correction: the deep lines are not capacity-short

An earlier version of this deck said the 6+6 line could make only 596 units against 616 demanded, and therefore that backorder was structural at 92%. That was wrong. 596 was the commander's output, not the line's physical ceiling. Driving every tool whenever the gates permit yields 640 units, so 616 is satisfiable at every depth.

The real statement is narrower and more interesting: the greedy heuristic leaves 44 units on the table at 6+6 versus 41 at 2+2, so its shortfall is roughly constant — but at 92% demand there is no longer slack to absorb it, which is why the commander's profit falls from $34,080 to $32,864 as the line deepens.

Result 3 — design findings

12 / 29

Three things that would have cost weeks to learn later

Exact forward beats relaxed forward

The fully-relaxed simulator (grad-soft) loses to the greedy heuristic on three of four patterns. Optimising a smoothed system optimises the wrong system, and rounding back to integers discards the gains. Straight-through — exact forward, surrogate backward — is the correct design.

The exact solver still wins — but slowly

CP-SAT beats the gradient on all four patterns, by $590 to $1,375. It needed 600 s to get there, and even then closed the optimality gap on none of them — 0.4% to 1.5% remained on a single-product, four-machine problem. At 180 s it had been beaten by random search. Solve time, not solution quality, is the variable to watch as the problem grows.

A live bug in the training reward

env.py sums four numpy booleans, and numpy boolean addition is logical OR — so the invalid-action count saturates at 1. With invalid_action_penalty=-2.0, an agent requesting four illegal starts is penalised exactly as much as one. Found by the port's equivalence test.

Relaxed forward degrades with depth

The bound is flat — read that carefully

$19,008 at all three, for two different reasons that should not be conflated. Revenue and total production cost are held fixed by choice (see below), so the ceiling should barely move. But the bound is also too loose to resolve the difference that does exist — see the capacity note.

Correction: capacity is not exactly constant

An earlier version of this deck said capacity was unchanged at 672 units. That holds for steady-state throughput — the bottleneck is a single diffusion stage at 0.25 units/tick no matter how many stages follow. But over a finite 2,688-tick horizon a longer pipeline loses real output to fill and drain. Measured, driving every tool whenever the gates permit:

2+2664 units · 12 tool-ticks per unit 4+4652 units · 24 tool-ticks per unit 6+6640 units · 36 tool-ticks per unit

So the true optimum should decline slightly with depth. The LP relaxation behind the dual bound can schedule fractionally and never feels that penalty, which is why the bound does not show it. Flat bound, genuinely non-flat optimum.

Why cost is held constant — a modelling choice, not a fact

Per-stage cost is set to 32/M for diffusion and 16/M for litho, so the total stays $24/unit at every depth and reproduces the original 8/4/8/4 at 2+2. Three options were considered:

OptionMargin/unitConsequence
Fixed cost & revenue (chosen)$56 flat Isolates lead time; rows comparable in dollars
Cost scales, revenue fixed$56 → $32 → $8 Measures collapsing economics, not scheduling
Both scale$56 flat Same experiment, more physically honest

The chosen option keeps the three rows directly comparable in dollars, which is what licenses reading the margin as +$606 → +$815 → +$1,363 rather than only as percentages. A 12-stage line at unscaled per-stage cost would run an $8 margin against $80 revenue, and family 3 would stop being a test of scheduling difficulty.

This inverts the 50% result and must be read alongside it

At 50% demand the gradient's margin grew with depth: +$606 → +$815 → +$1,363. At 92% it goes the other way — −$18 → −$751 → −$2,411 — and at 12 tools the gradient finishes below even the greedy commander. The pre-registered depth prediction held at low load and failed at high load. Both are real; neither cancels the other; the deck reports both.

A hypothesis was tested and rejected. Global-norm clipping at 100 and 1000 changed nothing: $31,350 / $31,380 vs $31,379 unclipped at 6+6. The Adam second-moment hypothesis was tested and rejected; this is not an optimiser-configuration artifact. Straight-through gradient norms reach 8.65e9 at 12 tools and 92% demand, so the natural explanation was Adam's second-moment estimate being poisoned by an enormous first gradient. Clipping should have fixed that. It did not, so the failure is in the gradient signal itself, not in how it is consumed.

Working hypothesis: the gradient needs slack

A start is gated by idle × canrun. When a tool is busy or starved the gate is closed and the derivative through that path is essentially uninformative. At 50% utilisation the gates are often open and the choice to idle is genuinely live, so the surrogate carries signal. At 92% with flat demand the line is saturated, gates are closed almost always, and the surrogate has little to say — while random sampling still finds good schedules by luck.

This also explains why bursty 92% (Alternating 92) still favours the gradient at +$398 while flat 92% does not: bursty demand leaves slack in the light weeks even when the average load is high. On this reading the operative variable is slack, not utilisation.

Why the mix decision is real

17 / 29

The two products are deliberately balanced

P1 pays more but consumes 25% more of the bottleneck. Measured in the only currency that matters — profit per unit of scarce capacity — they land within 2.9% of each other. Neither dominates, so "which product to run" is genuinely open at every decision point.

What "aligning the economics" could have destroyed

The obvious way to bring this line inline with the 1-product settings would be to flatten revenue and production cost to $80 / $24 for both products. That would have been a serious mistake. P1 would still take 25% longer on the bottleneck but earn the same margin, dropping it to $11.20 per bottleneck tick against P0's $14.00 — P0 dominating by 25%. The optimal policy would collapse to "always make P0" and the mix decision, the entire point of the two-product problem, would disappear.

So revenue, production cost and processing times were left untouched. Only the cost terms that carry no product differentiation were aligned.

Schedule anatomy

20 / 29

The two schedules solve the problem in opposite ways

Every table so far reports what each method scored. None of them says what the schedules actually do. Replaying both through the simulator and plotting every tick turns the $1,762 gap into something legible.

CP-SAT meters the line and builds ahead

It stages finished goods before each weekly spike and the amount escalates — 20, then 36, then 55 units as the demand pattern repeats. Mid-line inventory averages 6 units and ends at zero: every unit released gets converted and shipped.

The gradient pushes material and lets it pile up

Its staging goes the wrong way — 21, 18, 14 — so it never learns the pattern. It runs the diffusion tools harder (17% idle against 23.7%) and leaves 68 units stranded mid-line at the horizon, having averaged seven times the work-in-progress.

And it thrashes between products

The zoomed week at the bottom shows it: CP-SAT runs long single-recipe blocks, the gradient alternates constantly. On the litho tools that is 182 and 175 changeovers against 115 and 82.

The picture and the cost decomposition agree exactly

Both schedules ship identical revenue, $42,480. CP-SAT's $1,762 lead is three separate mistakes, each visible above: +$712 of production cost on units the gradient makes but never sells, +$640 of changeover cost from switching twice as often, and +$352 of holding cost on work-in-progress it leaves standing in the line.

There is a mechanistic explanation for the thrashing. The changeover cost is differentiable and the gradient does see it. What it cannot see is that switching now changes the tool's configuration state, and therefore what every later switch will cost — that path runs through a discrete variable held under stop_gradient. So the gradient prices each changeover in isolation and is blind to the sequence. Batching runs of one recipe is precisely the kind of decision that requires reasoning about the sequence.

And this should get faster

These are CPU numbers. A batched forward-and-backward pass over thousands of trajectories is precisely the workload GPUs exist for, while CP-SAT's branch-and-bound tree search gains little from one. The crossover should move further in the gradient's favour on the NVIDIA box — well founded, but untested.

Why this is the first result that looks deployable

Every earlier comparison ran on the solver's terms: unlimited time, full knowledge of demand, one schedule computed once. Under those conditions CP-SAT won seven configurations out of seven, and it deserved to.

Rolling-horizon replanning does not work that way. It needs a good schedule in seconds, repeatedly, as the state changes — not a proven-optimal one in half an hour. That is a regime where the gradient is already ahead, and it is the concrete form of the latency argument this programme has been asserting without evidence until now.

Why this run exists

The first two-product solves used 900 seconds — an arbitrary choice made to get a result inside a reasonable wait. The single-product depth study used 1800. An earlier version of this deck compared gaps across those different budgets and called the difference "8x more strain", which was not a sound comparison to draw.

This re-runs every mix at 1800 seconds with the same eight workers, so the only remaining difference between the two studies is the problem itself. The claim survives the matched test: the balanced mix sits at 8.4x the depth study's gap, the lopsided mixes at 3.7–4.5x.

Twice the time bought nothing

Gaps moved by −0.04, +0.24 and −0.64 percentage points — in both directions, which is run-to-run variance for a randomised parallel solver rather than convergence. None of the three was proven optimal.

That is the signature worth having. An under-resourced solve improves when given more time; a genuinely hard instance does not. Pipeline depth grew the model linearly and CP-SAT closed to 0.39% at this same budget. Setup variables grow it combinatorially, and the gap sits still.

Read it carefully: this is strain, not collapse

A gap is the distance between the best schedule CP-SAT has found and the best it can prove no schedule beats. It measures proof progress, not solution quality — the incumbent may already be optimal with the solver simply unable to certify it.

And CP-SAT still returns a better schedule than every learned method on every mix, by $733 to $1,700. A 1.4–3.3% unproven gap is entirely usable in practice. What has changed is that, for the first time in this programme, more compute stopped helping — which is the thing the scaling premise predicted and that pipeline depth conspicuously failed to show.

Next

26 / 29

Three moves, in priority order

1  ·  Test the assumption everything rests on

Scale CP-SAT up — more machines, then multiple products, then reentrant flows — and chart where solve time explodes. The entire strategic case depends on exact optimisation breaking at realistic scale, and that is currently backed by a complexity-class argument rather than a measurement. Roughly a week, and it either validates or kills the premise before anything expensive is built. The 180-second failures above suggest the knee may be closer than assumed.

2  ·  The closed-loop gradient test

Train a reactive policy by backpropagating end-of-episode profit through the dynamics. Straight-through only; gradient clipping is mandatory. Pre-registered kill criterion: if it cannot beat PPO's $31,493 on Alternating 92%, the pathwise route is dead for this problem and we stop.

3  ·  A cheaper hypothesis, in parallel

PPO's diagnosed failure is discounted local reward driving under-production. The tensorised environment now makes it cheap to run PPO at large batch with γ=1.0 and full-episode returns, which attacks that pathology directly — no differentiability required. It would be embarrassing to build a differentiable trainer and then find the discount factor was the answer.

Research potential

27 / 29

An honest assessment

Where there is little

GPU-vectorised environments, differentiable simulation, straight-through estimators, and RL for job-shop scheduling are all well-trodden. "We built a fast simulator" is engineering, not a contribution.

Where there is real potential

  • Differentiable discrete-event simulation. The literature is overwhelmingly rigid-body and contact physics. Production and queueing systems are a genuine gap.
  • Why it works here. Our discontinuities are multiplicative gates, not reflective contacts — so the linear pathway survives underneath the surrogate. That is a mechanistic explanation for beating the pessimism in the literature.
  • The reentrancy crossover. Charting where exact solvers break against where a learned policy still trains would be a real contribution — and it is move 1 above.

Overall: moderate, and concentrated almost entirely in the scaling question. On a four-machine toy the problem is too easy for any optimiser to distinguish itself decisively — which is exactly what the 90%-utilisation results show.
